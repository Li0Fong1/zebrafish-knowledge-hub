An AI-powered knowledge hub for potato
functional genomics
Dear Editor,
Potato is one of the most important tuber crops, feeding approx-
imately 1.3 billion people worldwide. Functional gene research is
fundamental for enhancing crop yield, quality, and resilience to
the rapidly changing global climate (Wang et al., 2025). Potato
functional genomics has advanced significantly in recent years
(Deng et al., 2024; Plunkert et al., 2024; Lian et al., 2025).
However, iterative updates to the potato reference genome
(DM1-3 516 R44) have introduced incompatible gene nomencla-
ture systems, creating persistent challenges in reconciling gene
IDs
and
symbols
across
historical
studies
(Supplemental
Figure 1). In parallel, an ∼100% increase in related publications
over the past decade has overwhelmed conventional literature
mining methods (Supplemental Figure 2). Collectively, these
issues are hindering progress in potato functional genomics.
The recent
emergence of large
language models (LLMs)
represents a paradigm-shifting advance in processing exabyte-
scale heterogeneous data (Yang et al., 2025; Zhang et al.,
2025). Subsequently, AI agent systems have been developed to
extend LLMs’ capabilities from cognitive assistance (‘‘knowing
how’’) to operational autonomy (‘‘getting it done’’), creating
transformative opportunities for addressing data-intensive chal-
lenges across diverse biological research domains.
To leverage LLMs and AI agents for advancing potato research
and to demonstrate their potential in functional genomics, we
present the Potato Knowledge Hub (https://www.potato-ai.top;
Figure 1), a freely accessible platform for the potato research
community. This integrated platform comprises three key
modules: (1) a potato knowledge database powered by a
retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) architecture, drawing
from over 6112 high-quality potato research articles, which over-
comes key limitations of general LLMs (static knowledge and
hallucination risk) to provide accurate scientific responses; (2) a
manually curated functional gene database based on the latest
potato genome assembly, featuring 2853 reported functional
genes with cross-version ID mapping; and (3) a practical toolkit
incorporating BLAST, ID conversion, gene annotation, and
Gene Ontology/Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes
enrichment analyses for efficient data processing. Moreover,
we developed the Potato Research Assistant, an AI agent that
integrates all platform modules to facilitate intuitive, natural
language-based interaction. The Potato Knowledge Hub is
designed to provide a streamlined user experience, freeing re-
searchers from labor-intensive literature mining and fragmented
analytical workflows. By substantially enhancing individual
research efficiency, this platform promises to accelerate discov-
eries across the field of potato functional genomics.
The first step in developing the Potato Knowledge Hub was to
construct a high-quality, specialized potato research corpus. To
comprehensively capture potato research, we searched NCBI
PubMed and the Web of Science Core Collection using ‘‘potato’’
as a keyword in titles or abstracts. This search, spanning
from January 1, 1900, to the present, retrieved over 57 795
nonredundant papers (Supplemental Figure 2). To enrich for
high-quality studies, we consulted the Chinese Academy of
Sciences
journal
ranking
and
extracted
research
articles
published in two top-tier journal categories (Q1 and Q2) in
biology, agriculture, and forestry sciences and multidisciplinary
categories, yielding 21 505 papers. We then implemented LLMs
to analyze titles and abstracts and to exclude studies that only
mentioned ‘‘potato’’ without using potato as the research
subject. This screening yielded 6007 studies relevant to potato
breeding, genetics, metabolism, physiology, genomics, and
functional gene research. We further manually reviewed report-
or letter-type papers lacking abstract information and retrieved
105 more papers. To assess database coverage, we manually
reviewed the 100 most recent publications in potato functional
gene research and confirmed that all were included. In total, we
collected 6112 high-quality potato research papers. To ensure
detailed and accurate content, we downloaded full-text Portable
Document Formats, rather than relying only on abstracts. Each
Portable Document Format was converted to plain text for down-
stream data processing (Supplemental Figure 3).
Paragraphs from each paper were analyzed using an LLM to
exclude nonscientific sections, including author information, affil-
iations, references, and acknowledgments. A RAG architecture
was applied to enhance response accuracy and reduce halluci-
nations. The full text of each paper was segmented and vector-
ized using an embedding model. To support multi-turn interac-
tions, an LLM analyzed the user query in its conversational
context and rephrased it to improve knowledge retrieval effi-
ciency. The rephrased query was vectorized using the same
model, and the top 200 relevant paragraphs were retrieved based
on embedding distance. A re-ranking model then rescored se-
mantic relevance between each segment and the query. The
highest-ranking
segments,
together
with
the
user
query,
were submitted to the LLM to generate the final answer
(Supplemental Figure 4A and 4B).
To determine the optimal chunking and retrieval settings that
maximize performance, we evaluated three chunking methods
(100 words with 60-word overlap, 200 words with 120-word over-
lap, and paragraph-based chunking) combined with three
retrieval depths (top 5, 10, and 20 chunks after re-ranking). We
collected 70 potato knowledge questions and corresponding
validated answers provided by professors, postdoctoral re-
searchers, and graduate students. We then employed an ‘‘LLM
as a judge’’ framework, which provides a scalable and interpret-
able proxy for human evaluation (Zheng et al., 2023). Specifically,
a third-party LLM (GPT-o3) acted as the judge and scored

genomes, together with incompatible gene
nomenclature systems, pose a persistent
challenge for reconciling gene IDs and sym-
bols
across
historical
studies
(Potato
Genome Sequencing et al., 2011; Pham et

1).
To
address
this
issue,
we
developed
an
LLM-driven
gene
entity
normalization workflow to accurately map
previously reported genes to the latest
genome assembly (DMv8.1). An LLM was
employed to analyze the full plain text of

independent rounds of extraction were per-

coverage. The merged results were then
subjected

yielding

also identified and corrected historical nomenclature errors (for
example, SP5G-B being mislabeled as SP6A; Herrera-Isidron
et al., 2024), with all corrections annotated and traceable in our
database. To ensure full transparency, our database maintains
direct links connecting DMv8.1 gene IDs, legacy identifiers, and

To optimize user interaction with the Potato Knowledge Hub, we
implemented an agentic task orchestration architecture and devel-
oped the Potato Research Assistant, an AI agent capable of
invoking the workflows of the knowledge database, gene data-
base, and integrated toolkit (Figure 1A). This agent interacts with
users via natural language, conducts intent analysis, and calls the
appropriate internal scripts to summarize potato research knowl-
edge, retrieve gene information and sequences, and execute spe-
cific analytical tools. For a queried gene symbol, the assistant can
summarize historical research, extract sequences (coding, protein,
and customized promoter regions, etc.), and provide detailed infor-
mation on putative gene function. By integrating these capabilities,
the AI agent liberates users from tedious literature mining and com-
plex bioinformatics software, enabling scientists without special-
ized computational expertise to efficiently explore and utilize a
tremendous amount of gene information with ease.
To evaluate the performance of the Potato Research Assistant, in
addition to the 70 questions used to benchmark the knowledge
base, we collected an additional 20 questions focused on gene un-
derstanding and 10 questions related to sequence extraction to
assess the gene database. We compared the responses generated
by the Potato Research Assistant with those produced by state-of-
the-art general LLMs; specifically, the open-source models
DeepSeek-R1-0528 and Qwen3-235B-A22B (thinking enabled) as
well as the closed-source models Gemini 2.5 Pro Preview-0506
and Claude Sonnet 4. Responses from both the Potato Research
Assistant and the general LLMs were evaluated using the same
scoring system applied in the previous RAG evaluation. The Potato
Research Assistant outperformed the general LLMs across all three
evaluation categories. This performance advantage underscores
the effectiveness of our agentic task orchestration architecture
and specialized database design in enabling superior domain
expert
knowledge
mastery
compared
with
general-purpose
LLMs. For knowledge retrieval tasks, the assistant showed higher
performance stability, whereas general LLMs exhibited score polar-
ization, likely due to an incomplete training corpus (Figure 1C and
Supplemental Figure 5). For gene detail comprehension and
sequence
extraction,
the
assistant
demonstrated
clear
advantages, supported by its dedicated potato gene database
(Figure 1D and 1E and Supplemental Figures 8 and 9).
Our work addresses key challenges in potato functional geno-
mics research, which is representative of crops with high genetic
complexity and frequent genome version inconsistencies. The
Potato Knowledge Hub will be actively updated on a monthly ba-
sis to integrate the latest advances in potato research. We antic-
ipate that this hub will serve as a valuable community resource for
advancing potato functional genomics and providing crucial
information to support potato breeding. Furthermore, the tech-
nical strategies developed here, including the LLM-driven gene
entity normalization workflow and the agentic task orchestration
architecture, offer a transferable framework for developing similar
platforms in other areas of biological research.

METHODS 
40 
Processing literatures 
41 
Potato-related literature was retrieved by searching the titles or abstracts from the Web 
42 
of Science Core Collection and PubMed, with “Potato” as the keyword. Relevant 
43 
studies, including their titles, abstracts, journal information, and DOIs, were exported 
44 
for subsequent filtering. Papers published in Q2 or higher-ranked journals, according 
45 
to the CAS Journal Ranking (2023), were retained as high-quality literature. 
46 
Furthermore, the title and abstract of each paper were then submitted to a LLM to screen 
47 
out papers that merely mentioned “potato” in the title or abstract but were not focused 
48 
on potato research fields. The screening was performed twice using DeepSeek-R1-0528 
49 
(671B) and Qwen3-235B-A22B (enable thinking), separately. To retain studies more 
50 
comprehensively, we kept studies that were present in the results from either screen. 
51 
Literature lacking abstract information underwent further manual review. This 
52 
collection was designated as high-quality potato literature. Full PDFs of these selected 
53 
studies were downloaded, and their plain text was extracted using MinerU (v1.2.2), 
54 
preserving the original texts organization (Wang et al., 2024). 
55 
 
56 
Potato literature knowledge database construction 
57 
To improve the quality of data retrieval, we performed data cleaning to remove the text 
58 
that was not related to the scientific content in the literature. We submitted each 
59 
paragraph of an article to Qwen3-235B-A22B to filter out authors, affiliations, 
60 
references, acknowledgements, standalone section titles, page headers, footers, as well 
61 
as formulas. The vectorization model “text-embedding-v4”, developed by the Qwen3 
62 
group, was employed to vectorize the collected literature into 2048 dimensions. Each 
63 
paragraph was treated as an individual chunk for this process. The vectorized data, 
64 
original 
text 
and 
DOI 
were 
stored 
in 
Chroma 
database 
(1.0.13, 
65 
https://github.com/chroma-core/chroma). 
66 
 
67 
For data retrieval, the DeepSeek-V3-250324 model was used to rephrase and 
68 
optimize user’s query for vector retrieval, for example, understand user intent based on 
69 

multi-turn conversation contexts, add abbreviations to full form phrases or expand 
70 
abbreviations. The query was vectorized using the same parameters and used to search 
71 
the Chroma database using the query() function. The top 200 chunks exhibiting the 
72 
highest similarity scores were extracted. To further identify the most relevant chunks, 
73 
the reranking model BGE-reranker-v2-m3 was utilized to assess the relevance between 
74 
these retrieved chunks and the query. The user’s query, along with the top relevant 
75 
chunks, was then sent to the Qwen3-235B-A22B (enable thinking) for summarization. 
76 
 
77 
Potato gene database construction 
78 
To extract gene IDs and their corresponding symbols, the plain text of each paper was 
79 
submitted to DeepSeek-R1-0528 (671B) and Qwen3-235B-A22B (enable thinking) for 
80 
analysis separately. The resulting data were subsequently merged. All gene ID and 
81 
symbol correspondences were then manually verified to correct potential inaccuracies. 
82 
To anchor reported gene IDs to the DMv8.1 reference genome, pairwise synteny 
83 
analyses were conducted between DMv8.1 and other potato genomes (RH89-039-16, 
84 
ATL_v3, Jan v1.1, SolTub3.0, DMv4.03, and DMv6.1) using MCscan within the JCVI 
85 
toolkit (v1.3.6) to identify orthologs (Tang et al., 2024). For genes where syntenic 
86 
orthologs could not be identified, and for gene sequences sourced from the Sol 
87 
Genomics Network, UniProt, NCBI GenBank, and the Agria genome, orthologs in 
88 
DMv8.1 were identified using the NCBI BLAST+ toolkit (v2.16.0) with default 
89 
parameters and a sequence identity threshold of >90%. Finally, gene IDs, symbols, 
90 
reported IDs, and their corresponding reference papers were compiled into an SQLite 
91 
database for efficient data querying. 
92 
 
93 
InterPro domain annotation, uniport similarity and expression analysis of each 
94 
gene 
95 
For comprehensive functional domain annotation and expression analysis of protein-
96 
coding genes in the DMv8.1 genome, we performed the analysis in the following steps. 
97 
First, protein sequences (with stop codons removed) were analyzed using InterProScan 
98 
(v5.74-105.0) to identify functional domains, and the corresponding InterPro (IPR) 
99 

identifiers and domain descriptions were extracted (Jones et al., 2014). In parallel, 
100 
homology searches were conducted using blastp (v2.14.0+) against the UniRef100 
101 
database, retaining only high-confidence matches (E-value < 1×10-10) and selecting the 
102 
top five hits with the highest sequence identity for functional inference (Suzek et al., 
103 
2015). For transcriptomic profiling, publicly available potato RNA-seq datasets were 
104 
retrieved from the NCBI SRA database (PRJNA573826). Raw sequencing reads were 
105 
processed for quality control using fastp (v0.24.0), then mapped to the DMv8.1 
106 
reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.1.0). Transcript abundance was quantified using 
107 
StringTie (v2.2.3) and normalized as Transcripts Per Million (TPM) (Chen, 2023; Kim 
108 
et al., 2015; Pertea et al., 2016). For samples with biological replicates, the mean TPM 
109 
value across replicates was used to represent the final expression level of each gene, 
110 
ensuring robust quantification.

AI agent and Potato knowledge hub construction 
113 
The “Potato Research Assistant” agent is powered by the Doubao-seed-1.6 LLM (with 
114 
thinking 
enabled), 
which 
was 
accessed 
directly 
via 
its 
API 
115 
(https://www.volcengine.com/). We employed the model as a frozen reasoning engine 
116 
without conducting any further continuous pre-training or fine-tuning. The model acts 
117 
as a reasoning engine designed to analyze user intent via natural language 
118 
understanding, invoke external databases or tools for information retrieval, and 
119 
synthesize the final answer based on the context. The Potato Knowledge Hub was 
120 
developed using the Flask framework (v3.1.0, https://flask.palletsprojects.com/). 
121 
Python was used to write the backend logical scripts, while HTML and JavaScript were 
122 
employed for developing the web-based user interface.

Tools development 
135 
The BLAST tool was developed based on the NCBI BLAST+ toolkit (v2.16.0) 
136 
(Camacho et al., 2009). The Annotator and ID converter were built using local Python 
137 
scripts. Gene Ontology (GO) annotations were retrieved in OBO format from the OBO 
138 
Foundry (http://purl.obolibrary.org/obo/go/go-basic.obo), while KEGG pathway 
139 
annotations were extracted from the built-in TBtools.KeggBackEnd database in 
140 
TBtools-II (Chen et al., 2023). The gene annotation file for the DMv8.1 genome was 
141 
generated using the online platform eggNOG-mapper (http://eggnog-mapper.embl.de/) 
142 
with default parameters. After establishing the correspondence between the annotation 
143 
file and the background files, functional enrichment analysis was performed using the 
144 
enricher function from the R package ClusterProfiler (v4.12.6), with the significance 
145 
threshold set to pvalueCutoff = 0.05 and adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg 
146 
correction (Wu et al., 2021). The enrichment results were visualized using the ggplot2 
147 
package (v3.5.2) (Wickham, 2016).

149 
Performance evaluation 
150 
One hundred questions covering potato knowledge queries (70), gene detail 
151 
comprehension (20), and sequence extraction (10) were collected from professors, 
152 
postdocs, and graduate students, and the corresponding reference answers were 
153 
generated and curated manually. We defined a 100-point assessment system consisting 
154 
of four dimensions: knowledge accuracy (maximum 40 points), completeness 
155 
(maximum 30 points), relevance (maximum 20 points) and clarity (maximum 10 
156 
points). We also carefully defined the judgment rules for each dimension. The LLM-as-
157 
a-judge framework was developed based on the reasoning model GPT-o3 (with 
158 
reasoning_effort set to “high”). The questions, verified answers, meticulous judgment 
159 

rules, as well as the corresponding answers from the LLMs and Potato Research 
160 
Assistant were submitted to GPT-o3 for scoring. Although the LLM-as-a-Judge 
161 
framework offers scalability, we acknowledge that LLMs may not be entirely impartial 
162 
and can be susceptible to biases inherent in their training or prompting (Krumdick et 
163 
al., 2025; Szymanski et al., 2025). To mitigate these potential limitations, we utilized 
164 
strictly defined judgment rules and expert-validated standard answers as the ground 
165 
truth for all evaluations. The complete benchmark dataset—benchmark questions, 
166 
manually curated standard answers, responses from both the Potato Research Assistant 
167 
and general LLMs, judgment criteria, individual scores, and brief comments explaining 
168 
each 
response 
and 
score—has 
been 
deposited 
in 
Figshare 
169 
(https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.29815859).

Comparison of performance across different chunking strategies and retrieval 
172 
counts 
173 
To evaluate the impact of different chunking strategies and retrieval depth on LLM 
174 
generation performance, we compared three text segmentation granularities: fixed 
175

176

177

178 
70 potato knowledge queries from the above benchmark dataset and employed the 
179 
“LLM-as-a-judge” framework to automatically score the generation results for each 
180 
combination.

scoring 
data 
have 
been
